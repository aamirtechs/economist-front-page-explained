# The Economist Front Page, Explained

**Every famous Economist front page since 2000, explained in easy words: what the cover image shows, what it meant at the time, the prediction it made, and whether that prediction actually came true.**

The Economist is famous for its covers. One picture, a few sharp words, and a whole argument about the world. But the jokes, the wordplay and the old film and art references can be hard to decode, and nobody ever goes back to check whether the cover was right.

This project does both jobs. It explains the covers, and it marks them.

**62 covers explained so far, from 2000 to 2026, and growing.**

> **Start here: [The Predictions Board](predictions.md).** Every cover on one page, in a single table: the prediction on the left, and on the right its status, ✅ happened, 🟡 partly happened, ❌ did not happen, or ⏳ still pending.

## How each cover is explained

Every entry in this project follows the same seven steps:

1. **What the cover shows.** A plain description of the picture, including the joke or reference hidden in it.
2. **The picture, piece by piece.** Every element of the artwork decoded: what each person, object and symbol stands for.
3. **What was happening.** The news of that week, in a few easy sentences.
4. **The point it made.** The argument or warning the magazine was making.
5. **The verdict.** One of: *Came true*, *Partly true*, *Did not happen*, *Not a prediction*, or *Too early to tell*.
6. **What actually happened.** The evidence for the verdict, with sources.
7. **What happened next.** A short dated timeline of the events that followed the cover.

Key entries also carry original charts and timelines drawn for this project, so you can see the numbers behind the verdict: house prices for the 2005 bubble warning, the oil price crash of 2014 to 2016, China's growth after the 2015 scare, world food prices in 2022, and more.

Start with the [Predictions Scorecard](scorecard.md) for the greatest hits and the famous misses, or jump to any year below.

## How to read an Economist cover

Five things that unlock almost every cover:

- **The joke is the argument.** A snail carrying the Earth means world trade has slowed to a snail's pace. A brick labelled "house prices" falling from the sky means a crash is coming. Decode the picture and you have the article.
- **Watch the question marks.** "Europe's saviour?" and "Has Brazil blown it?" are doubts, not claims. The magazine often asks on the cover and answers inside.
- **Wordplay carries the verdict.** "Acropolis now" (a film title plus Greece), "Skintland" (Scotland with no money), "Ruination Day" (Trump's "Liberation Day" tariffs, renamed). If the pun is cruel, the article is too.
- **A cover with no words is the loudest kind.** The bleeding Ukraine flag of March 2022 said more than any headline could.
- **Beware the cover curse.** Traders joke that a cover story marks the top or bottom of a trend. Sometimes it is true: "Brazil takes off" (2009) came just before Brazil's fall. Sometimes it is not: the housing warning of 2005 was three years early and exactly right. This project checks each one instead of guessing.

## A note on the images

The cover artwork belongs to The Economist, so this project does not copy the images. Instead, every entry describes the cover picture in detail, in words, and links to sources where you can see the original. You can browse the real covers in the magazine's own archive at [economist.com](https://www.economist.com).

This is an unofficial study and fan project. It is not connected to The Economist, and the explanations and verdicts are this project's own work, written from the public record. Where a cover's exact words or artwork could not be verified from open sources, the entry says so honestly rather than guessing.

## Contributing

Corrections are welcome, especially exact cover lines and dates. If you can verify a cover that is missing, open an issue with the source. The rules: easy words, no invented covers, every verdict backed by what actually happened.
