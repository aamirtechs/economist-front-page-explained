## "Has the Arab spring failed?": July 13, 2013

**What the cover shows:** The cover is built around the big question in its headline, tied to a special report on the Arab world. (A reliable description of this cover's artwork could not be confirmed from open sources, so this entry does not guess at it. You can see the original in The Economist's cover archive.)

**The picture, piece by piece:**
- The headline question itself: here the words do the work of the picture, asking readers to judge two years of uprisings.
- The word "spring": it recalls the hopeful name given to the 2011 uprisings, the season of new life after a long winter of dictators.
- The question mark: the magazine refuses to answer yes on the cover. Its answer inside was "not yet".
- A honesty note: because the original artwork could not be confirmed, this entry decodes the words rather than guessing at the picture.

**What was happening:** Egypt's army had just removed the elected president, Mohamed Morsi, days earlier. Syria was deep in a civil war, and Libya was falling into chaos, so the hopes of 2011 looked broken.

**The point it made:** The magazine's own answer was "not yet". It argued that despite the blood and the setbacks, the Arab peoples had woken up and change would be a long process, so the world should not give up hope.

**Verdict: Partly true.** The hopeful answer mostly did not come true in politics: Egypt returned to military rule, Syria, Libya and Yemen fell into long wars, and even Tunisia, the one success, later slid backwards. The magazine's point that people's minds had changed for good is harder to test and may still matter in the long run.

**What happened next:**
- July 3, 2013: Egypt's army removed the elected president, Mohamed Morsi, just days before this issue appeared.
- May 2014: the army chief, Abdel Fattah el-Sisi, became Egypt's president, and military rule was back.
- Through the 2010s: Syria, Libya and Yemen fell into long and bitter civil wars.
- 2021: even Tunisia, the one success story, slid backwards as its president seized more power for himself.

## "Has Brazil blown it?": September 28, 2013

**What the cover shows:** The same Christ the Redeemer statue from the famous [2009 cover](2009.md), but this time nosediving back down towards the ground like a crashed rocket. The magazine was openly making fun of its own earlier optimism.

**The picture, piece by piece:**
- The same Christ the Redeemer statue: readers are meant to remember the 2009 rocket cover at once. This is its sequel.
- The nosedive: the rocket of 2009 is now a crashed plane, falling head first back towards the ground.
- The joke is on the magazine itself: The Economist is mocking its own earlier optimism, a rare public U-turn.
- The question in the title: "Has Brazil blown it?" asks whether a golden chance was wasted, not whether the chance ever existed.

**What was happening:** Brazil's growth had stalled, inflation was rising, and in June 2013 millions of Brazilians had protested in the streets about poor public services and the cost of the coming World Cup.

**The point it made:** Brazil had wasted its boom years with bad policies, too much state control and too little reform.

**Verdict: Came true.** Brazil fell into its worst recession on record in 2015 and 2016, the giant Petrobras corruption scandal exploded, and President Dilma Rousseff was removed from office in 2016.

**What happened next:**
- 2014: Brazil hosted the World Cup, and the giant Petrobras corruption scandal began to explode.
- 2015 to 2016: Brazil fell into its worst recession on record.
- August 2016: President Dilma Rousseff was removed from office by Congress.
- Later years: the scandal reached almost every major party and business in Brazil.

*Source: [Everand: Brazil, The Troubled Rise of a Global Power](https://www.everand.com/book/1079595863/Brazil-The-Troubled-Rise-of-a-Global-Power)*
